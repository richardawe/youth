I
Part One

What Is AI?

1440, Mainz

A goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg built a machine that could press ink onto paper faster than any scribe could write by hand.

Within fifty years, the printing press had spread across Europe. It gave ordinary people a Bible in their own language — and it also fed a flood of propaganda, forged documents, and religious wars.

The press itself was neutral. What mattered was the hearts of the people who used it.

Tonight

We're standing at a similar hinge point. Except this time the "press" doesn't just copy words — it can write them, argue them, and increasingly act on them.

That machine is artificial intelligence.

The question isn't whether AI will change the world — that's already happening. The question is: what kind of people will we be while it happens?

A working definition

Software that learns patterns — and now increasingly acts on them.

Artificial intelligence is software that learns patterns from enormous amounts of data and uses those patterns to do things that used to require human judgment: writing, translating, recognizing faces, diagnosing illness, predicting outcomes, and now taking multi-step actions on our behalf.

A picture worth keeping
Think of AI less like a calculator and more like an apprentice who has read almost everything ever written — but has never lived a single day of human life. It can imitate wisdom without possessing it. It can produce comfort without ever having grieved.

That gap — between fluency and understanding — is the single most important thing to grasp about this technology.

Two waves, one shift

From oracle to worker

Generative AI

Tools like ChatGPT and Claude that produce text, images, code, and conversation when you ask.

Agentic AI

The newer wave: AI that doesn't just answer, but takes multi-step actions — booking, coding, managing workflows, negotiating on our behalf.

This is the shift from AI as an oracle you consult, to AI as a worker that acts — and it raises the stakes considerably.

A story, not a statistic
A friend of mine runs a small customer service team. Eighteen months ago she had twelve people. Today she has four — and an AI system that handles the routine tickets. The four who remain are paid more and do more meaningful work. Eight people had to find a new way to make a living.

AI didn't destroy her business. It transformed it. And transformation always has a cost, paid by someone.

III
Part Three

Opportunities & Risks

Genuinely, in fairness

What AI makes possible

Amplified capability

A rural clinic can access diagnostic support once reserved for major hospitals. A small nonprofit can draft proposals and analyze data like a full research team.

Time returned

Repetitive, draining administrative work can be handed off — freeing people for the relational and creative work only humans can do.

Access and inclusion

Translation, literacy support, and assistive tools are giving voice to people previously locked out of full participation.

Genuinely, in fairness

What AI puts at risk

Economic disruption

Real displacement is falling hardest on those with the least cushion to absorb it, even where the long-run transition proves net-positive.

Truth and trust erosion

Convincing misinformation, fake images, and cloned voices at a scale no prior technology allowed. When anything can be faked, the temptation is to trust nothing.

Concentration of power

A small number of companies and nations now shape systems mediating information and opportunity for billions.

The discipleship question

As AI grows emotionally fluent — patient, affirming, always available — what happens to community and dependence on God?

Every risk and opportunity on the last two slides is, underneath the technology, a question about what we worship, what we trust, and who we believe we are.

Which means this isn't just a technology conversation. It's a faith conversation.

IV
Part Four

What Does Christian Faith Say?

Genesis 1:27

Our creativity reflects our Maker.

Humanity is made imago Dei — in God's image. One implication is creativity itself: we build, shape, and invent because our Creator does. AI is, in that sense, a fruit of the image of God in us.

But the image of God is relational and moral, not merely functional. Intelligence was never the whole of what it means to be human. A being can be extraordinarily capable and still be nothing like God — because capability was never the point. Love, faithfulness, and relationship were.

Genesis 11

"Let us build a tower… and make a name for ourselves."

A story about technological achievement, human unity, and ambition — turned toward self-glorification instead of God's purposes. God didn't condemn building itself; He interrupted a project where human power had outrun human wisdom and humility.

A striking caution for anyone racing to build or adopt ever more powerful systems: capability is not wisdom, and "because we can" is not "because we should."

Wisdom literature

Technology tests the heart. It doesn't replace it.

Proverbs is obsessed with one question — not what do you know, but what do you love, and whom do you trust?

"Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding." — Proverbs 3:5

AI will increasingly offer understanding on demand. The danger isn't using the tool — it's quietly transferring our trust from God to the tool.

Genesis 2:15

Not fear. Not idolatry. Stewardship.

Fear

"This is dangerous — avoid it entirely."

Idolatry

"This will solve everything — maybe even save us."

Scripture offers a third way. Humanity is placed in the garden "to work it and take care of it" — dominion exercised as service, not domination. Use it. Shape it. Set boundaries around it. Hold it loosely enough that it never becomes what we worship.

The test that doesn't change

Love God. Love neighbor.

A practical filter for every use of AI: does this make me more loving, more truthful, more present to the people around me — or less?

  • An AI tool that frees you for unhurried time with your family passes that test.
  • An AI companion that quietly replaces real relationships does not.
  • An AI that helps a small church serve its community better passes the test.
  • An AI-generated sermon with none of the pastor's own prayer behind it should give us pause.
V
Part Five

How Can We Get Started?

For individuals
  • Get literate, not just opinionated. Spend thirty minutes this week actually using an AI tool. Fear and hype both thrive on ignorance.
  • Set a personal rule of trust. Decide in advance what you'll never outsource — prayer, real discernment, comforting a grieving friend.
  • Bring it to community, not just a search bar. Test what AI tells you against Scripture and against people who know you.
For families & churches
  • Talk with your kids before the culture does. Chatbots, companionship apps, homework tools — name them openly at home.
  • Form a small team at church — not just "the tech person" — to ask where AI could free staff and volunteers for more relational ministry, and where you should deliberately choose the slower, human way.
  • Teach on this from the pulpit. Silence leaves people to be discipled by the technology instead of by Scripture.
Back to Mainz, 1440

The printing press didn't determine whether Europe would have a Reformation or a century of wars — the hearts of the people who held it did.

AI will not determine whether the next fifty years are marked by flourishing or fracture. That will be decided by whether the people building it, using it, and being shaped by it are rooted in something — Someone — bigger than the machine.

"Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding." Proverbs was written three thousand years before anyone imagined a machine that could think. It has never been more relevant than tonight.
